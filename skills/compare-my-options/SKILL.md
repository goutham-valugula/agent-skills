# Compare options against what matters

1. Restate the decision in one sentence and list the options being considered.
2. Extract the user's must-haves, preferences, budget or time limits, and deal-breakers. Do not assume priorities that were not given; use a short set of reasonable criteria and label them if details are missing.
3. Separate known facts, user preferences, estimates, and unknowns. For facts that may have changed—or choices involving significant time or money—research current authoritative sources and link them.
4. Compare each option against the same criteria. Explain the biggest trade-off in plain language; do not hide a weak point behind a total score.
5. Recommend one option only if the evidence supports it. State what new fact would change the recommendation.
6. Give one practical next step, such as a question to verify or a low-cost trial.
7. Check that the conclusion follows from the user's stated priorities and the evidence.

## Output

Give the recommendation first, then a compact comparison, the main uncertainty, and the next step. Avoid false precision and scores that imply more certainty than the inputs support.

## Boundaries

For medical, legal, or financial decisions, explain general trade-offs and questions to raise with a qualified professional; do not make the decision for the user. Never accept terms or commit money on the user's behalf.

## Example request

“Compare these two options for my needs: low setup time matters most, I can spend up to $50 a month, and I need it to work on my phone. Tell me which you’d choose and why.”
